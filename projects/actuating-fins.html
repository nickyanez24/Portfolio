Active Rocket Roll Control

Ongoing Project
Current stage: First full-vehicle CAD iteration

What the project is

I'm leading the mechanical development of a subscale active roll-control rocket with my team in Longhorn Rocketry. The vehicle uses four servo-actuated canards and is intentionally focused on roll instead of trying to solve roll, pitch, and yaw at the same time. We're still developing the vehicle, and I'm currently working through the first full-vehicle CAD iteration.

Where it started

The project started with servo-actuated tabs built into the main fins. I worked on early vehicle modeling, CAD, actuator packaging, and physical prototypes. That work exposed a practical problem: the servo was buried inside the fin, and the team still needed a good way to remove and reuse it. We discussed an access or casing idea, but never developed it into a finished design.

One prototype had, taken inspiration from an Instagram video I came accross where a printed internal lattice was filled with epoxy. We tried recreating this to see how the part would turn out. It was extremely rigid and extremely heavy. The mass would have shifted the vehicle CG downward which would have decreased stability, but the part was never load tested, aerodynamically tested, or flown.

How the architecture changed

After working through the old fin-tab direction, I decided to push the project toward a dedicated canard-based roll-control vehicle. The team moved forward with that direction, and moving the actuators away from the main fins gave us a cleaner way to deal with packaging and serviceability. It also gave me a separate vehicle for working through the mechanical and controls problems.

I wanted the first version to stay small and focus only on roll. The goal is to prove the active-roll-control setup on a subscale rocket before trying to scale it to a larger vehicle. That gives me a faster and cheaper way to iterate without carrying as much system complexity into every change.

I first considered two canards because fewer servos meant simpler hardware. I moved to four because the symmetric layout gives the controller more options when the rocket is not perfectly aligned with the flow. I still need to calculate how much roll authority the canards actually provide.

Current design

I designed the current subscale vehicle in OpenRocket for the team, working through the overall vehicle sizing, motor selection, mass and stability, and baseline flight configuration. It has four forward servo-actuated canards, four stationary aft fins, and active control in roll only. The baseline uses an F67-6 motor. The rocket is 3.1 inches in diameter, 28.85 inches long, about 36.3 ounces loaded, and sits at about 1.38 calibers of static stability. The model I built predicts a maximum velocity of about 195 ft/s, which I later used in my servo torque calculation.

Packaging has already changed the size of the rocket. An earlier concept used an airframe around 2.6 inches in diameter and looked workable before the actual servos were brought into CAD. Once I placed the real hardware inside the vehicle, the available space became impractical. I had to resize and rework the layout around the components the rocket will actually carry.

By creating and working through the CAD model earlier I have been able to already make better informed desgin decisions to ensure a successful rocket and canard design is in place.

Current CAD and where the project stands

The first complete external vehicle model is now built. The nose, airframe, four forward canards, and four aft fins are all in the assembly, so the overall architecture is finally starting to look like a real vehicle instead of a collection of separate mechanisms.

The mechanical integration is still underway. The canard attachment hardware and internal canard bay are not finished, and I'm waiting on some dimensions before I can finalize those pieces. That interface and the internal packaging are the main CAD work right now.

Checking the servo

We already had the Corona DS-239MG servos. The servo torque specs looked like plenty for a vehicle this size, and once I had the current vehicle modeled in OpenRocket and knew its predicted maximum velocity, I could use that flight condition and the actual canard geometry to check whether that assumption was reasonable.

This did not select the servo, and is simply a first order verification of our servo choice. I was checking one thing, whether the servo looked obviously undersized at the highest loading from the current vehicle simulation.

Assumptions for the first-pass check

  • Pressure: 1 atm
  • Temperature: 311 K
  • Maximum velocity: 195 ft/s
  • Maximum canard deflection: 15°
  • Force coefficient: CN ≈ CL
  • Lift slope: ideal 2D thin-airfoil value
  • Load location: quarter-MAC
  • Aerodynamic load factor: 3×

I used 1 atm to push density and aerodynamic load higher than they should be at the elevated launch site. I used the ideal 2D lift slope for the same reason: the real low-aspect-ratio canard should produce less force. Quarter-MAC is only an estimate of where that force acts, and the 3× multiplier is a conservative preliminary choice, not a formal requirement.

Because the canard is tapered and swept, I used the mean aerodynamic chord. The root chord is 2.50 inches, the tip chord is 1.00 inch, and the span is 1.50 inches, so the taper ratio is 0.40.

MAC = (2/3)[Cr + Ct - (Cr * Ct) / (Cr + Ct)] ≈ 1.86 in

yMAC = (h/3) [(1 + 2(Ct / Cr) / (1 + (Ct / Cr)] ≈ 0.643 in

The swept leading edge puts the MAC leading edge about 0.600 inches aft of the root leading edge. Taking quarter-MAC gives a simple load location about 1.06 inches aft of the root leading edge. The servo axis is at about 1.40 inches, so the torque arm is 0.336 inches, or 0.00853 m.

At 15 degrees, the ideal thin-airfoil value gives CL ≈ 1.6 and CN ≈ CL for the actual check. The real canard has an aspect ratio around 0.86 and should produce less force than this ideal 2D model. I used the ideal value on purpose because it pushes the servo load higher without adding an uncertain finite-wing correction.

ρ = P / (R T) ≈ 1.14 kg/m³

q = ½ ρ V² ≈ 2.00 kPa

CL = 2π α ≈ 1.645  →  CN = 1.6

F = q S CN ≈ 5.50 N per canard

T = F d ≈ 0.0470 N·m

With the 3× load multiplier, which is simply a safety factor, the design torque comes out to 0.141 N·m per servo.

What this tells me

The Corona DS-239MG is rated for about 0.392 N·m of stall torque at 4.8 V and 0.451 N·m at 6.0 V. Compared with the 0.141 N·m design torque, that leaves about 2.8× and 3.2× torque margin. These are margins against published stall torque, not factors of safety or continuous-torque ratings. The servo looks reasonable for now, so I'm keeping it and moving forward with bench testing.

The idealized lift coefficient also points to the next problem. The real canard should produce less lift force, which helps the servo but may hurt control authority.

What happens next

The immediate mechanical work is to finish the canard attachment, internal bay, and overall layout so I can manufacture the hardware. Then I can prove the canard actuation on the bench and integrate the sensing and control electronics.

After that comes IMU roll-rate measurement, closed-loop testing, system-level testing, and eventually a subscale flight. It is still an active project, and I'll keep updating the page as the design turns into hardware and real test results.
