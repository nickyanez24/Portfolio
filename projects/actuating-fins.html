Rocket Controls

Active Rocket Roll Control

Designing and building a subscale rocket to demonstrate active roll control using servo-actuated canards.

Ongoing Project Current stage: First full-vehicle CAD iteration

What I'm trying to build

A focused flight-control demonstrator

Right now, the goal is intentionally narrow: build a subscale rocket that can demonstrate active roll control using four servo-actuated canards. This is not an attempt to jump straight into full three-axis control. I want to work through one control problem all the way from vehicle architecture and aerodynamic control authority to actuator sizing, mechanical packaging, avionics integration, manufacturing, bench testing, and eventually flight testing.

Design progression

How this project got here

The project did not start with the current canard layout. The original concept used servo-actuated tabs built into the rocket's main fins. During that phase, I worked through OpenRocket vehicle models, aerodynamic loading and actuator torque calculations, servo selection, fin geometry, CAD, additive manufacturing, and physical fin prototypes.

That architecture raised a practical question that was still unresolved when the project changed direction: how do we remove and reuse a servo after it has been embedded inside an assembled fin? We discussed casing and access concepts that could make the actuator serviceable, but we did not take one of them through a final design. The fin-tab work was still useful—it made actuator packaging and serviceability real design concerns instead of details to solve later.

From there, the project evolved toward actuating the full control surface and eventually toward a dedicated canard-based vehicle. I do not view that as the first concept failing. The scope and architecture changed as we learned more about what would have to fit together mechanically and what kind of vehicle would let us demonstrate the control problem clearly.

An early lesson in overbuilding

One early fin prototype was 3D printed with an internal lattice, and I let epoxy seep into that structure as a reinforcement experiment. I mainly wanted to see how the manufacturing approach would turn out physically. The result was extremely rigid, but it was also ridiculously heavy—basically a brick. Putting that much mass low on the rocket would have shifted the vehicle's center of gravity significantly downward.

The fin was never load tested, aerodynamically tested, or flown. It was a manufacturing prototype, but it made the trade between stiffness, mass, manufacturability, and vehicle-level effects very tangible.

Project scope

Why a dedicated demonstrator

The current vehicle is scoped around proving one thing well: active roll control. Trying to address roll, pitch, and yaw at once—or placing experimental hardware directly onto a larger competition vehicle—would make it harder to tell what is working and what needs to change.

A smaller demonstrator keeps the control problem isolated, gives me room to iterate the hardware faster, and should make the eventual flight-test results easier to learn from before the system grows into something more complicated.

System layout

Current architecture

The current concept is a subscale rocket with four forward-mounted, servo-actuated canards and four stationary aft fins. Active control is focused on roll, with onboard sensing and control electronics planned for closed-loop operation. Recovery and avionics also have to fit into the vehicle as parts of the same architecture, not as separate additions at the end.

Planned sensing, control, avionics, and recovery

I originally considered using two canards because that would reduce the hardware count and mechanical complexity. I selected four instead because they give the vehicle a more symmetric control layout and a better way to deal with aerodynamic moments when it sees an off-axis angle of attack. That decision is architectural at this stage; I am not claiming quantitative control authority before the analysis and testing support it.

Packaging has also changed the vehicle itself. An earlier airframe concept was about 2.6 inches in diameter. Once I started placing the actual servos in CAD, it became clear that fitting the mechanism inside that diameter would be a problem. The vehicle layout had to be resized and reworked as the hardware became less abstract.

Current CAD

The first complete vehicle-level model

Right now I'm working through the first full CAD iteration. The nose, airframe, four forward canards, four stationary aft fins, and overall external vehicle architecture are modeled. The canard attachment hardware and internal canard bay are not complete yet; I am waiting on some dimensions before I can finish those components.

The next mechanical work is centered on those interfaces and the internal packaging. Having the full vehicle in CAD now gives me a way to find integration problems while the design is still easy to change.

Tradeoffs and integration

Engineering decisions so far

Four canards vs. two

Two canards would reduce the part count, but four create a symmetric control architecture and give the system a better way to respond to moments that appear with off-axis angle of attack.

Vehicle packaging

The earlier 2.6-inch concept stopped looking practical once the real servo envelope entered CAD. Instead of forcing the mechanism into that layout, I reworked the vehicle around the hardware it actually needs to carry.

A dedicated roll demonstrator

Restricting the first objective to roll control reduces the number of interacting problems and creates a clearer path toward hardware iteration, bench work, and an eventual flight test.

Serviceability matters

The embedded-servo fin concept showed how quickly actuator access and reuse can become part of the mechanism design. The current packaging work keeps those practical integration questions visible.

Full-vehicle CAD early

Modeling the entire rocket before every mechanism is finalized exposes conflicts among the control hardware, avionics, recovery, and airframe while moving things around is still cheap.

Systems thinking

What I'm learning

The biggest lesson so far is that a locally good solution can still create a vehicle-level problem. A very stiff fin can add unacceptable mass. A compact actuator concept can make the servo impossible to service. A vehicle diameter that works in OpenRocket can stop working once real hardware enters CAD.

I am also seeing how useful it is to reduce scope without losing the real engineering problem. Active roll control still requires the mechanical, aerodynamic, controls, avionics, and recovery decisions to work together, but the dedicated demonstrator gives me a practical way to work through those interfaces one iteration at a time.
