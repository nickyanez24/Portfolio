Active Rocket Roll Control

Ongoing Project
Current stage: First full-vehicle CAD iteration

What the project is

I'm building a subscale rocket that can actively control its roll using four servo-actuated canards. For now, the project is intentionally limited to roll instead of trying to solve roll, pitch, and yaw at the same time. The vehicle is still in development, and I'm currently working through the first full-vehicle CAD iteration.

Where it started

The project originally used servo-actuated tabs built into the rocket's main fins. I worked on the OpenRocket vehicle model, aerodynamic loading and torque calculations, servo sizing, fin geometry, CAD, 3D printing, and several physical prototypes. That work gave us an initial picture of the loads the actuator would need to handle and what it would take to package the mechanism inside a fin.

One practical issue we had not finished solving was serviceability. The servo was embedded inside the fin, so after assembly we would still need a way to remove it and reuse it. We discussed a removable casing or some type of access feature, but the project changed direction before we developed that into a final design.

One of the early fins was printed with an internal lattice, and I let epoxy seep into the structure to see how the manufactured part would turn out. It became extremely rigid, but it was also extremely heavy—basically a brick. That much mass near the bottom of the rocket would have shifted the vehicle's center of gravity significantly downward. The prototype was never load tested, aerodynamically tested, or flown. I only manufactured it to see how the concept behaved physically.

How the architecture changed

From there, the project moved toward fully actuated surfaces and eventually the current canard-based vehicle. As the design developed, actuator access, packaging, and integration started driving more of the architecture.

For the current vehicle, we narrowed the goal to roll control. That keeps the first flight system manageable while still forcing us to work through the mechanical hardware, sensing, controls, and integration needed to actually close the loop.

I initially considered using two canards because fewer servos meant less hardware and simpler packaging. I moved to four because the symmetric layout gives the controller more options for countering aerodynamic moments when the rocket is not perfectly aligned with the flow. How much control authority that gives the vehicle still needs to be worked out through analysis and testing.

Current design

The current vehicle has four forward servo-actuated canards and four stationary aft fins. The canards are only being used for roll control. Onboard sensing and control hardware are planned for closed-loop operation, and the avionics and recovery hardware have to fit into the same vehicle rather than being treated as separate systems at the end.

Packaging has already changed the size of the rocket. An earlier concept used an airframe around 2.6 inches in diameter and looked workable before the actual servos were brought into CAD. Once I placed the real hardware inside the vehicle, the available space became impractical. I had to resize and rework the layout around the components the rocket will actually carry.

That is one reason I'm modeling the full vehicle this early. The CAD is not just a drawing of a finished idea; it is where conflicts among the canard mechanism, airframe, avionics, and recovery layout start becoming visible.

Current CAD and where the project stands

The first complete external vehicle model is now built. The nose, airframe, four forward canards, and four aft fins are all in the assembly, so the overall architecture is finally starting to look like a real vehicle instead of a collection of separate mechanisms.

The detailed mechanical integration is still underway. The canard attachment hardware and internal canard bay are not finished, and I'm waiting on some dimensions before I can finalize those pieces. That interface and the internal packaging are the main CAD work right now.

Checking the servo

The current servo is the Corona DS-239MG, and we had already ordered the servos before I did this calculation. The original choice was based mostly on the direction of the design, the available packaging space, the advertised torque, and the fact that it seemed like it should be enough for a small subscale rocket. Once the OpenRocket model and canard geometry were far enough along, I finally had enough real numbers to go back and check that choice.

The question was simple: at the highest aerodynamic loading predicted for this vehicle, does the servo still have enough torque to hold and move the canard? This is only a first-order screening calculation. It is not CFD, a measured hinge moment, or final servo validation. I just wanted to know whether the servo was obviously undersized before continuing with it.

OpenRocket predicts a maximum velocity of 195 ft/s, or about 59.4 m/s, for the current F67-6 baseline. That is the velocity I used for this check. For the atmosphere, I used 311 K to match the hot launch conditions already used in the vehicle simulation and 1 atm for pressure. Using 1 atm instead of reducing the pressure for launch-site elevation gives a higher density, which pushes the dynamic pressure, aerodynamic force, and predicted servo torque upward. With R = 287 J/(kg·K), the ideal gas relation gives a density of about 1.135 kg/m³.

ρ = P / (R T)  →  ρ ≈ 1.135 kg/m³

q = ½ ρ V²  →  qmax ≈ 2.0 kPa

To turn the aerodynamic force into torque around the servo shaft, I needed an approximate location for that force. I used the quarter-chord of the mean aerodynamic chord as a reasonable first-order estimate rather than pretending I knew the exact center of pressure. With the current canard geometry, that puts the load about 1.064 inches aft of the root leading edge. The servo axis is about 1.400 inches aft of the same reference, leaving a 0.336-inch moment arm.

The canard has a very low aspect ratio, about 0.86. I initially looked at applying a finite-wing correction to the ideal thin-airfoil lift slope of 2π rad−1, because the real canard is obviously not an infinite two-dimensional airfoil. Its actual lift-curve slope should be lower. For this servo check, though, adding a correction meant adding span-efficiency and low-aspect-ratio assumptions that I did not need. I used the higher ideal 2D value instead, which pushes the estimated aerodynamic load in the conservative direction.

For the current preliminary design check, the maximum canard deflection is 15 degrees. At that angle, ideal thin-airfoil theory gives a lift coefficient of about 1.645. I rounded that to 1.6 and treated the normal-force coefficient as approximately equal to it for this calculation. That does not mean the real canard has a CL of 1.6; it is the intentionally high first-order coefficient used to screen the actuator.

CL = 2π α  →  CL ≈ 1.645, rounded to CN = 1.6

F = q S CN  →  F ≈ 5.4 N per canard

T = F d  →  Taero ≈ 0.047 N·m

I applied a 3× aerodynamic load factor because I wanted meaningful margin on top of a simple model before comparing it with the servo. That factor is a preliminary design choice, not a certification requirement or a value taken from a standard. With that factor included and the calculation rounded conservatively, the maximum design torque is 0.141 N·m per servo.

What this tells me

The 0.141 N·m design torque leaves about 2.8× margin against the published stall torque at 4.8 V and 3.2× at 6.0 V. Those are torque margins, not structural factors of safety, and stall torque is not continuous operating torque. This does not fully validate the servo. It tells me the DS-239MG is not obviously undersized in this first-order check and is reasonable to carry into bench testing.

The calculation also sets up the next question. The real low-aspect-ratio canard should generate less aerodynamic force than the ideal 2D model, which helps the servo. But that same force is what creates roll moment. Now that the servo looks viable, I need to quantify whether the canards are actually large enough to produce the roll authority the vehicle needs.

What happens next

The immediate mechanical work is still to finish the canard attachment, internal bay, and overall layout so the hardware can be manufactured. The next aerodynamic and controls work is the control-authority calculation: estimating the roll moment from the canards and comparing it with what the vehicle needs.

After that, I plan to prove the canard actuation on the bench, integrate the sensing and control electronics, validate the IMU roll-rate measurement, and work toward closed-loop testing. Once the mechanical and controls work can operate together, the project can move into system-level testing and eventually a subscale flight. It is still an active project, and I'll keep updating this page as the design becomes hardware and the testing starts producing real results.
