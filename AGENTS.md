# Portfolio Development Instructions

## Portfolio Purpose

This repository contains the personal engineering portfolio of Nickolas Yañez, an Aerospace Engineering Honors student at The University of Texas at Austin pursuing a minor in Robotics.

The portfolio is intended primarily for aerospace, space, robotics, controls, flight systems, GNC, simulation, systems engineering, and related engineering recruiting.

The goal is NOT to make Nick sound more accomplished than he is.

The goal is to accurately show:
- genuine technical interest
- engineering thought process
- hands-on experience
- technical ownership
- design iteration
- systems thinking
- lessons learned
- progression as an engineer

A recruiter should leave the portfolio understanding not only what Nick worked on, but how he thinks through engineering problems.

## Voice and Writing Style

This is extremely important.

The portfolio should sound like Nick explaining engineering work he genuinely cares about.

Writing should be:
- natural
- technically grounded
- conversational when appropriate
- enthusiastic without being exaggerated
- confident without pretending expertise that has not been earned
- understandable to an engineering recruiter who may not know every detail of the project

First-person writing is encouraged.

Technical terminology should be used when it communicates real engineering information.

Do NOT automatically rewrite Nick's writing into corporate or academic language.

Avoid generic AI/corporate phrases such as:
- leveraged
- spearheaded
- cutting-edge
- robust solution
- innovative solution
- utilized in order to
- multidisciplinary synergy
- successfully implemented

Avoid turning project descriptions into résumé bullets disguised as paragraphs.

Avoid making every sentence sound polished to the point that the author's personality disappears.

Occasional informal phrasing is acceptable when it makes the explanation feel genuine, provided the page remains appropriate for an engineering portfolio.

## Technical Honesty

Never invent:
- test results
- dimensions
- performance numbers
- design requirements
- completed milestones
- engineering decisions
- reasons for engineering decisions
- project outcomes

Never describe planned work as completed work.

Clearly distinguish among:
- completed work
- work currently in progress
- planned future work

If technical information is missing or ambiguous, do not guess.

Instead:
1. preserve the uncertainty,
2. omit the claim if it is unnecessary, or
3. flag it for Nick to clarify.

Do not create a cleaner engineering story by inventing failures or motivations that did not actually occur.

Real engineering iteration is valuable even when a previous concept was not proven wrong.

## Engineering Storytelling

Project pages should prioritize engineering reasoning over lists of accomplishments.

When appropriate, communicate:

1. What problem was being addressed
2. Why the project interested Nick
3. What Nick personally owned or contributed
4. Initial concepts or approaches
5. Important engineering decisions
6. Why those decisions were made
7. How the design changed over time
8. Analysis used to support decisions
9. CAD, simulation, manufacturing, or testing evidence
10. Current project status
11. Remaining work
12. Lessons learned

Do not force every project into this exact structure.

Each project should tell its own story.

Design changes should be presented honestly. A change in architecture does not automatically mean the previous architecture failed.

## Active / Ongoing Projects

Some projects in this portfolio are actively being developed.

For ongoing projects, the portfolio should document engineering progression rather than pretending to present a finished product.

Use language such as:
- "I'm currently working on..."
- "The current iteration..."
- "The next step is..."
- "This design has evolved from..."
when appropriate.

An unfinished project can still demonstrate strong engineering ability if the reasoning and progression are documented well.

## Evidence Over Claims

Whenever possible, show engineering work rather than simply claiming it.

Useful evidence includes:
- CAD
- engineering drawings
- OpenRocket models
- MATLAB/Python plots
- simulations
- trade studies
- calculations
- test setups
- prototypes
- manufactured hardware
- photos
- diagrams
- videos

Pages should use visuals to break up technical explanations and help communicate progression.

Do not add decorative images simply to make a page look fuller.

## Portfolio Organization

The homepage should remain focused and recruiter-friendly.

Not every project Nick has completed needs to appear on the homepage.

Featured projects should emphasize the strongest evidence of:
- flight hardware
- controls
- robotics/autonomy
- simulation/dynamics
- systems engineering
- integration/testing

Additional projects may remain accessible without being featured.

## Visual Design

Preserve the existing overall visual identity:
- dark aerospace-inspired theme
- orange accent color
- clean engineering-oriented presentation

Prefer:
- strong hierarchy
- readable text
- generous spacing
- meaningful project visuals
- responsive layouts
- simple interactions

Avoid:
- unnecessary animations
- excessive visual effects
- clutter
- huge walls of text
- generic technology-themed graphics

The site should feel like an engineer's portfolio, not a startup landing page.

## Mobile Responsiveness

Any website modification must preserve usability on:
- desktop
- tablet
- mobile

Do not assume desktop-only viewing.

## Existing Content

Do not delete project pages, images, calculations, or other engineering material simply because they are no longer featured.

Older work may still be useful for:
- documenting design progression
- supporting project pages
- preserving engineering history

If something appears obsolete, determine whether it should simply stop being displayed rather than deleting the underlying material.

## Repository Workflow

Do not make substantial portfolio changes directly on main unless Nick explicitly requests it.

For meaningful changes:
1. work on an agent/* branch,
2. make the requested changes,
3. inspect the resulting diff,
4. check for broken HTML/CSS and links,
5. summarize what changed,
6. push the branch for review,
7. do not merge into main until explicitly approved.

Small fixes may be handled differently only when Nick explicitly requests them.

Do not modify unrelated pages while working on a specific project.

## Collaboration

Nick is the final decision-maker for technical facts, engineering intent, and portfolio direction.

Codex should handle implementation and make reasonable coding/layout decisions without requiring Nick to micromanage HTML or CSS.

When given a detailed content brief, implement it rather than repeatedly asking about minor visual decisions.

However, never make up missing engineering information simply to complete a page.

Another ChatGPT conversation may be used by Nick to develop content strategy, reconstruct project history, and review changes after they are pushed to GitHub.

GitHub should be treated as the shared source of truth for implemented portfolio content.

## Current Portfolio Direction

The portfolio is being intentionally reduced from a large collection of projects into a smaller number of deeper engineering stories.

Depth is more valuable than project count.

The strongest pages should make it possible to understand:
- what Nick built or analyzed,
- what decisions he made,
- why he made them,
- what evidence supported those decisions,
- what changed during development,
- and what he learned.

Do not optimize the portfolio simply to sound impressive.

Optimize it to make the engineering work understandable, credible, and memorable.
